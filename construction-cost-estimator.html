A Starting Number Before the First Contractor Call

Before an architect gets involved or a contractor sends a formal quote, most people building a home want a rough sense of the total budget they're working toward. This calculator turns a covered area and a local per-square-foot rate into that starting figure, with an optional split into material and labor shares and a contingency buffer on top.

Building the Estimate

  1. Enter covered area: Total built-up area in square feet.
  2. Enter your local rate per square foot: Based on current construction costs in your area and quality tier.
  3. Optionally split material and labour percentages: For a rough cost breakdown alongside the total.
  4. Set a contingency percentage: A buffer added on top for the unexpected.
  5. Read the result: Base cost, optional breakdown, and a final budget figure with contingency included.

What's Actually Being Calculated

The core calculation is simple multiplication: covered area times your entered rate per square foot gives the base cost. If material and labour percentages are set, that base figure is split proportionally between them for a rough directional breakdown — useful for budgeting conversations, though not a substitute for an itemized quote. The contingency percentage is then applied on top of the base cost and added to it, producing a final recommended budget figure that has some cushion built in for the inevitable surprises a construction project brings.

Worked Example: An 1,800 Sq Ft Home

Take a home with 1,800 square feet of covered area, at a local rate of PKR 2,800 per square foot, with a 60/40 material-to-labour split and a 10% contingency. Base cost comes to PKR 5,040,000. Split proportionally, that's roughly PKR 3,024,000 for material and PKR 2,016,000 for labour. Adding the 10% contingency (PKR 504,000) on top of the base cost brings the final recommended budget to PKR 5,544,000 — the number worth carrying into early conversations with a contractor or lender, rather than the unpadded base figure.

Where This Number Gets Used

Someone planning a new home build gets a rough starting budget before consulting an architect for a detailed design and quote. A property developer projects total costs across several similar unit sizes for a housing scheme. Someone weighing whether to build versus buy an existing property estimates what a comparable new build would actually cost, as a reference point against listed prices. Loan applicants use the estimate to gauge roughly how much construction financing they'll need to request before approaching a bank formally.

Getting the Rate Figure Right

The rate per square foot is the single biggest lever in this calculation, and it varies enormously by region, construction quality tier, and time period — research current local rates carefully rather than relying on an outdated figure from a year or two ago, since material and labor costs shift meaningfully over time. Decide clearly whether the rate you're using reflects a grey structure (foundation, walls, roof, no finishing) or a fully finished home, since these can differ substantially, and the estimate only reflects whichever standard the entered rate assumes.

What This Estimate Can't Replace

This is a top-line planning figure, not a substitute for a detailed, itemized quote from a contractor — actual costs depend on specific material choices, finishes, site conditions, structural design complexity, and local labor rates in ways a single blended rate can't capture. Treat the output as a useful starting point for early budget conversations, and always get a proper written quote before making financial commitments based on this number alone.

Comparing Build Cost Against Buying

This estimate is often most useful as a comparison point rather than in isolation — run it alongside listed prices for existing comparable properties in the same area to see whether building genuinely comes out cheaper once land, permits, and time are factored in, or whether an existing property at a similar price actually offers better value. Building typically takes longer and carries more uncertainty than buying, so a build-versus-buy decision should weigh this cost estimate against that time and risk difference, not just against the raw price tag of an existing home.

Runs Entirely in Your Browser

Every figure entered is calculated locally using client-side JavaScript — nothing is uploaded, logged, or sent to a server at any point.

What's a typical construction rate per square foot to use as a starting point?

This varies enormously by region and quality tier — check recent local project costs or ask a few local contractors for current rates rather than relying on a generic figure.

Should I use the grey structure rate or the finished construction rate?

Decide based on what you're planning to budget for; a grey structure rate covers the shell only, while a finished rate includes flooring, fixtures, paint, and other finishing work.

How much contingency should I actually budget on top of the base estimate?

10% is a common starting point for a straightforward project; more complex builds, older properties, or projects in areas with volatile material costs often warrant 15-20%.

Does the material and labour percentage split apply universally across regions?

No, the actual split varies by region, labor cost structure, and project type; adjust the percentages to match typical local proportions rather than using a fixed default.

Why does this estimate differ from a contractor's formal quote?

A formal quote accounts for specific site conditions, material brands, structural details, and finishing choices this rough per-square-foot calculation can't capture.

Is this estimate useful for renovation projects too?

It's designed for new construction; renovation costs depend heavily on existing structure condition and scope, making a per-square-foot rate less reliable for that use case.
