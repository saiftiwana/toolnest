Construction Bricks Calculator

Estimate bricks needed for a wall (standard 9"×4.5"×3" with mortar) — free, instant.

Ordering the Right Number of Bricks the First Time

Under-ordering bricks means a second supplier trip mid-build, often at a slightly different shade from a different production batch — visible in the finished wall forever. Over-ordering ties up cash in material sitting unused on site. This calculator works out a realistic number from wall dimensions using standard brick sizing, so the order you place matches what the wall actually needs.

Steps to Get an Estimate

  1. Enter wall length and height: Both in feet, for the total wall face you're building.
  2. Set wall thickness: In inches — 9 inches is a standard single-brick-thick wall.
  3. Set a wastage allowance: A percentage added for breakage and cutting during construction.
  4. Read the breakdown: Total wall area, base brick count, and a recommended purchase quantity including wastage.

How the Count Is Worked Out

The calculation starts from a standard brick face of 9 by 3 inches, plus a half-inch mortar joint on each side, to work out how many bricks fit into one square foot of wall face. That figure gets multiplied by your total wall area, and by the number of brick layers your entered thickness implies — a 9-inch wall is one layer, an 18-inch wall is two. The result is the base brick count before any wastage is added.

Worked Example: A 20×8 Foot Boundary Wall

Take a boundary wall 20 feet long and 8 feet high, built at the standard 9-inch thickness, with a 5% wastage allowance for breakage. Total wall area comes to 160 square feet. At roughly 4.33 bricks per square foot of face (accounting for the standard mortar joint), the base count works out to 693 bricks. Adding 5% wastage for breakage and cutting brings the recommended purchase quantity to 728 bricks — that extra 35 bricks is cheap insurance against a mid-wall supply run. The same calculation also estimates the mortar volume needed to fill the joint spacing across the full wall, giving a rough second material figure alongside the brick count.

Who Reaches for This Before Ordering

Contractors run this before placing a bulk order with a brick supplier, especially on a wall size they haven't priced out before. Homeowners handling a DIY boundary wall or garden wall project use it to get a purchase number without calling a contractor first just for an estimate. Suppliers occasionally use it to sanity-check a customer's stated requirement against an independent number. On larger housing developments, estimators run the same calculation across several identical unit walls to project total material needs for the phase.

A Few Things Worth Knowing

Brick dimensions genuinely vary by manufacturer and region — some markets use a slightly larger or smaller standard brick than the 9×4.5×3 inch size this calculator assumes, so if your local supplier's bricks differ noticeably, treat the result as a close estimate rather than an exact figure. Share the number with your supplier early in the process so they can confirm stock is available before your construction timeline depends on it. And always keep the wastage allowance in the final purchase number — a wall that comes up 20 bricks short near the top course is a frustrating, avoidable delay.

Where the Estimate Can Be Off

This tool doesn't account for door and window openings in the wall — for a wall with openings, subtract their combined area from the total wall area before running the calculation, or the estimate will overstate what's needed. It also assumes a standard running-bond brick pattern; unusual bonds or decorative brickwork with more cutting will need a higher wastage allowance than the 5-10% typically recommended for a plain wall.

Comparing Against a Supplier Quote

If a brick supplier gives you a quantity that differs noticeably from this calculator's number, it's worth asking what assumption they're working from before assuming either figure is wrong. A supplier padding for a known regional wastage rate, quoting for a slightly different brick size, or including bricks for an adjoining structure not part of your wall measurement can all explain a gap. Walking through your wall dimensions together against this tool's breakdown — total area, base count, and wastage separately — usually resolves the difference quickly and gives you a clear basis to negotiate or confirm the order.

All dimensions you enter are processed directly in your browser using JavaScript — nothing is sent to a server, stored, or logged, so there's no account needed to get an estimate.

Does this calculator work for hollow blocks or only solid clay bricks?

It's calibrated for standard solid clay bricks at 9×4.5×3 inches; hollow concrete blocks are typically larger and would need a different per-square-foot brick count.

How do I adjust the estimate for a wall with door and window openings?

Subtract the combined area of all openings from your total wall area (length times height) before entering it, since the calculator otherwise assumes a solid wall face.

What wastage percentage is realistic for a straightforward rectangular wall?

5-7.5% typically covers normal breakage and minor cutting; complex layouts with more corners or decorative patterns often need closer to 10%.

Does a double-thickness wall really need exactly double the bricks?

Close to it — the calculator rounds thickness into whole brick layers, so an 18-inch wall calculates as two full 9-inch layers rather than a fractional multiplier.

Can I use this for an interior partition wall instead of a boundary wall?

Yes, the same area-and-thickness math applies to any rectangular brick wall, interior or exterior.

Is the mortar volume estimate accurate enough to order mortar materials separately?

It's a rough figure based on joint spacing and useful for a ballpark, but for an exact mortar material order, the Cement & Concrete Volume Calculator gives a more detailed mix-ratio breakdown.
