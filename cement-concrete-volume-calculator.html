From Slab Dimensions to a Material Order

A concrete pour needs cement, sand, and aggregate in specific proportions — get the ratio right but the total volume wrong, and you either run short mid-pour or pay for material that sits unused. This calculator takes your slab, column, or footing dimensions and your chosen mix ratio and turns them directly into a cement bag count plus sand and aggregate volumes.

Using the Calculator

  1. Enter length and width: The footprint of your slab, footing, or structural element, in feet.
  2. Set the thickness: In inches, for the depth of the pour.
  3. Pick a mix ratio: 1:2:4 for standard use, 1:1.5:3 for stronger structural elements, or 1:3:6 for lean, less load-bearing work.
  4. Read the breakdown: Wet volume, dry volume, and the cement, sand, and aggregate quantities that ratio produces.

Why Dry Volume Isn't the Same as Wet Volume

Wet concrete volume is simply length times width times thickness — the physical space the poured slab occupies. But dry cement, sand, and aggregate take up more space before mixing than the finished wet concrete does, because water fills the gaps between dry particles during mixing and compaction. The standard adjustment for this is a 1.54 multiplier applied to the wet volume, giving the dry volume figure that's then split across cement, sand, and aggregate according to your chosen mix ratio's proportions.

Worked Example: A 15×12 Foot Slab at 4 Inches

Take a slab 15 feet by 12 feet, poured 4 inches thick, using the standard 1:2:4 mix ratio. Wet volume works out to 60 cubic feet. Applying the 1.54 dry-volume multiplier gives 92.4 cubic feet of dry material to source. Split across the 1:2:4 ratio (7 total parts), that's 13.2 cubic feet of cement — roughly 10.6 bags at the standard 1.25 cubic feet per 50kg bag — plus 26.4 cubic feet of sand and 52.8 cubic feet of aggregate. Switch to the stronger 1:1.5:3 ratio for the same slab and the cement quantity rises proportionally, since a stronger mix uses relatively more cement per unit of dry volume.

Who This Calculation Serves

Contractors run it before placing a material order for a slab pour, checking the numbers against a supplier's quote. Site engineers use it to independently verify that a quoted material list matches the actual structural requirement. Civil engineering students see the standard dry-volume-multiplier concept applied to real numbers rather than left as an abstract formula in a textbook. Quantity surveyors cross-check a contractor's submitted bill of materials against an independent calculation before approving a purchase order.

Getting a Dependable Order

Always order a little more than the exact calculated amount — spillage during mixing and transport, plus minor measurement variance in the actual pour, mean the theoretical number and the practical need rarely match exactly. Confirm your local cement bag weight before finalizing an order; this calculator assumes the common 50kg bag, but some regions and suppliers use a different standard size, which would shift the bag count without changing the underlying cement volume.

Where This Estimate Has Limits

This calculation gives material volumes for a straightforward rectangular pour — for a slab with a non-rectangular footprint, break it into rectangular sections and calculate each separately, then sum the results. It also doesn't account for reinforcement steel volume displacing some of the concrete volume, which on heavily reinforced structural elements can make a small but real difference to the actual concrete needed.

Choosing Between the Three Ratios

The 1:2:4 ratio is the default for a reason — it's the general-purpose mix used across most residential slabs, footings, and non-critical structural work, and it's what most local suppliers will assume unless told otherwise. The 1:1.5:3 ratio trades a lower sand-and-aggregate share for a higher cement share, producing a denser, stronger mix suited to columns, beams, and other load-bearing elements where extra strength matters more than cost. The 1:3:6 lean mix goes the other direction, stretching the cement further across more sand and aggregate for cost-sensitive, non-structural work like a plinth fill or a mass concrete bed where load-bearing strength isn't the priority. Picking the wrong one in either direction either wastes cement on the lean end or under-strengthens a load-bearing element on the other.

Which mix ratio should I use for a residential slab foundation?

1:2:4 is the standard ratio for most residential slabs and general structural use; reserve 1:1.5:3 for elements bearing significantly more load.

Why does the cement quantity change so much between mix ratios for the same slab?

The ratio determines what fraction of the total dry volume is cement versus sand and aggregate; a 1:1.5:3 mix allocates a larger share to cement than a 1:3:6 lean mix.

Does the 1.54 dry-volume multiplier apply to every type of concrete pour?

It's the standard, widely used multiplier for typical construction concrete; specialized mixes with different aggregate types may use a slightly different factor.

How many 50kg cement bags equal one cubic foot?

One 50kg cement bag is roughly 1.25 cubic feet in volume, which is the conversion this calculator uses to turn cement volume into a bag count.

Should I calculate columns and footings the same way as a flat slab?

Yes, the length x width x thickness volume calculation and mix ratio math apply the same way regardless of the structural element's specific role.

What if my slab has an irregular, non-rectangular shape?

Split it into rectangular sections, calculate the material needed for each section separately using this tool, then add the totals together.
