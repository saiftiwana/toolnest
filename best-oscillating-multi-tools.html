Buyer's Guide: How to Choose an Oscillating Multi-Tool

Six things that decide whether you bought the right one — in the order they should decide it.

01

Platform first, tool second

A bare tool is only cheap if its battery is already on your shelf. Price every pick as tool + battery + charger before comparing: on this page the DEWALT and CRAFTSMAN kits include both, while the Bosch, Makita and Milwaukee need a platform you already own to make sense. Buying into a new platform for one tool is the most expensive mistake in this category.

02

Oscillation angle is cutting aggression

The angle is how far the blade sweeps: this lineup runs from the Makita’s 3.2° to the Milwaukee’s 4.2°. A wider angle removes material faster and vibrates more; a narrower angle cuts slower but cleaner and with more control. Match it to your work — tear-outs want angle, finish work wants restraint.

03

Speed range and how you control it

Every pick here tops out at 18,000–20,000 OPM, but control differs: a dial (Makita, CRAFTSMAN, Milwaukee) holds a set speed, while the DEWALT’s variable-speed trigger feathers speed mid-cut under a 3-speed cap. Sanding and scraping reward low, steady speeds; cutting rewards the top end. The control style you prefer is a legitimate reason to pick one tool over another.

04

The blade mount decides your running costs

Blades are the consumable you will buy for years. Open OIS-style mounts (DEWALT, Makita, Milwaukee, CRAFTSMAN) take cheap blades from every brand, and DEWALT’s universal adaptor widens that further. Bosch’s StarlockPlus grips firmer and changes fastest, but Starlock blades cost more. Neither is wrong — just budget for the shelf your mount shops from.

05

Brushless buys runtime, not magic

Brushless motors (DEWALT, Bosch, Milwaukee) run longer per charge, hold speed under load and never need brush changes — DEWALT quotes up to 57% more runtime than a similar brushed tool. Brushed designs (Makita, CRAFTSMAN) are proven and cheaper. Heavy users feel the difference daily; weekend DIYers mostly feel it in the price.

06

Vibration is the comfort spec

An oscillating tool works by shaking, and your hands absorb what the tool does not. Bosch quotes up to 80% less vibration than its previous generation; Milwaukee tunes for low vibration too. If your jobs are long — grout removal, floor sanding, scraping — weight this higher than a small speed difference. Numb hands make sloppy cuts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an oscillating multi-tool actually used for?

It is the problem-solver of remodeling. The blade vibrates side to side thousands of times a minute instead of spinning, so it can plunge straight into drywall or flooring, flush-cut a door jamb so new flooring slides under it, cut out grout between tiles, scrape old adhesive, sand into corners a normal sander cannot reach, and trim pipes and nails in tight spots. It is rarely the best tool for one big job — it is the only tool that can do twenty small ones.

Should I buy a kit or a bare tool?

If you already own batteries from one of these brands, buy that brand's bare tool — it is the cheapest route and the battery is the expensive part. If you own nothing, buy a kit: on this page the DEWALT and CRAFTSMAN kits include a battery and charger, while the Bosch, Makita and Milwaukee are bare tools that cannot cut anything until you add a battery and charger, which can add $60–$100 to the real price.

Which oscillating multi-tool is best for a first-time buyer?

The DEWALT DCS356C1 kit. It is a complete package — brushless tool, battery, charger and starter blades — with the strongest owner record in the category (4.8 stars from 12,694 ratings), and its 3-speed selector plus variable-speed trigger is the easiest speed control here to learn on. If the budget is tight, the CRAFTSMAN kit is the other complete package and costs less.

Do blades and accessories fit across brands?

Mostly, with one big exception to know. DEWALT, Makita, Milwaukee and CRAFTSMAN use open (OIS-style) accessory mounts, and DEWALT's universal adaptor fits most other brands' accessories too. Bosch's StarlockPlus mount grips its blades in 3D and changes them fastest, but Starlock blades cost more than generic OIS blades and you should budget for Bosch-fit accessories. Standard Starlock blades fit the Bosch tool; the larger StarlockMax blades do not.

Is brushless worth it on an oscillating tool?

Usually, yes. Brushless motors (the DEWALT, Bosch and Milwaukee here) run longer per charge, hold their speed better under load and have no brushes to wear out — DEWALT quotes up to 57% more runtime than a similar brushed tool. Brushed tools (the Makita and CRAFTSMAN) cost less and are proven designs; for weekend DIY pace the difference is runtime and price, not whether the job gets done.

Can an oscillating multi-tool cut metal?

Yes, with the right blade. Fit a bi-metal blade and it cuts nails, screws, copper pipe and steel strapping — slowly, but in places no other saw reaches. Wood blades will be ruined by metal, so swap blades for the material. For long cuts in metal, a reciprocating saw is faster; the oscillating tool wins when the cut is short, precise or in a corner.
