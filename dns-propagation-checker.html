About DNS Propagation

When a DNS record changes, different resolvers around the world update at different times because each caches the previous answer until its TTL (Time To Live) expires. That's why the same domain can briefly show different results depending on which resolver you ask \u2014 it's expected during a transition, not necessarily a mistake.

Tips

DNS propagation isn't a single global event with one finish line — each resolver independently caches your previous record until its TTL expires, so seeing different results from Google versus Cloudflare briefly after a change is expected, not necessarily a problem. If a change seems stuck longer than its TTL should allow, double-check the record was actually saved correctly at your DNS provider before assuming it's a caching delay.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does DNS propagation typically take after update?

It depends entirely on the previous record's TTL — a resolver won't check for a new value until its cached TTL expires, so a record with a 24-hour TTL can take up to 24 hours to update everywhere, while a 5-minute TTL updates much faster.

Why does DNS take time to propagate globally?

Every DNS resolver caches answers independently for the TTL duration to reduce load on DNS servers, so there's no single moment when a change is 'live everywhere' — it depends on when each resolver's cache expires.

How to check DNS propagation across multiple locations?

This tool checks Google Public DNS and Cloudflare DNS directly; comparing their results gives a useful signal even without checking every resolver worldwide.

What does it mean when DNS propagation is complete?

It means the resolvers you've checked are consistently returning your new value with no leftover old records or mismatches.

How to troubleshoot DNS propagation issues?

Check the Diagnostics section after running a check — it explains specific situations like NXDOMAIN, SERVFAIL, or resolver disagreement in plain language based on the actual responses received.

What are A records, MX records, CNAME records, and TXT records?

A records point a domain to an IPv4 address, MX records specify mail servers, CNAME records alias one domain to another, and TXT records hold arbitrary text often used for verification or email authentication.

Should I wait for full DNS propagation before going live?

For most changes, waiting until your key resolvers show consistent results reduces the chance visitors briefly see an old version of your site or service.

What is the difference between DNS propagation and DNS caching?

Caching is the mechanism (a resolver storing an answer for its TTL duration); propagation is the overall process of a change becoming visible everywhere as those individual caches expire and refresh.
