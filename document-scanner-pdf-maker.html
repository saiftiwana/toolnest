How It Works

This document scanner pdf maker tool uses perspective correction (the same underlying geometry used by dedicated scanning apps) to straighten a document photographed at an angle: you position four corner handles on the document's edges, and the tool mathematically warps that quadrilateral into a flat rectangle, correcting the keystone distortion that comes from photographing a flat page from an angle. From there, Grayscale, Black & White, and Enhanced filters process the straightened image for readability, and every page you add joins one combined PDF.

Key Features

As a scan document without upload tool, corner detection starts with a sensible default box that you drag into place — since no automatic edge detection is perfectly reliable across every lighting condition and background, manual adjustment with touch-friendly handles keeps results accurate for real-world photos. Black & White mode includes an adjustable threshold for scanned text, Enhanced mode sharpens text edges for crisper readability, and brightness/contrast sliders fine-tune results further. Multiple pages can be captured or uploaded, reordered isn't needed since pages appear in the order added, and each page supports independent rotation, duplication, or deletion before a single combined PDF is exported with your choice of page size, orientation, and quality.

Common Use Cases

Someone digitizing a stack of paper receipts photographs each one, corrects the perspective, and combines them into a single PDF for an expense report. A student scanning handwritten lecture notes uses Black & White mode with an adjusted threshold to make the pages print-ready and easy to read. Tips

Photograph documents in even lighting and directly enough that all four corners are visible in the frame — this makes dragging the corner handles into position faster and gives the perspective correction more accurate source pixels to work with. For handwritten or faint text, try Black & White mode and adjust the threshold slider while watching the live preview, since the right threshold varies with how the original page was lit when photographed.
